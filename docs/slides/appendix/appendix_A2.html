The Matrix Inverse

Section 1

What the Inverse Is

For a scalar, \(a^{-1} = 1/a\) undoes multiplication by \(a\) — as long as \(a \neq 0\).

Matrix inverse. For a square matrix \(\mathbf{A}\), the inverse \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) is the unique matrix satisfying \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}\).

Exactly one condition determines whether \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) exists — the one we already have:

\[ \mathbf{A}^{-1} \text{ exists} \iff \det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0 \iff \mathbf{A} \text{ is full rank} \]

Three ways of saying the same thing, from Part I. Today we compute the inverse itself.

Two Ways to Compute It

The adjugate formula. \(\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{\det(\mathbf{A})}\,\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})\) — exact, and useful for seeing why \(\det(\mathbf{A})=0\) breaks everything (division by zero, directly).

The adjugate formula needs a cofactor for every entry. For a \(4\times4\) matrix that is sixteen \(3\times3\) determinants — it does not scale. Software never uses it.

What software — and this course — uses instead: Gauss-Jordan elimination. Same row operations you already used to think about rank; now we push them all the way to a solution.

Gauss-Jordan: The Idea

Gauss-Jordan elimination. Augment \(\mathbf{A}\) with the identity, \([\,\mathbf{A} \mid \mathbf{I}\,]\), then apply row operations until the left block becomes the identity. Whatever the right block becomes is \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\):

\[ [\,\mathbf{A} \mid \mathbf{I}\,] \;\longrightarrow\; [\,\mathbf{I} \mid \mathbf{A}^{-1}\,] \]

Three legal row operations, same as always: swap two rows, scale a row by a nonzero constant, add a multiple of one row to another.

Gauss-Jordan: Worked Example (Part 1)

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(\mathbf{A}) = 1 \]

\[ \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 3 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \]

\[ \begin{aligned} &: \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 3 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \\[4pt] R_1 \to R_1 - R_2 &: \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} -1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 4 & 3 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \\[4pt] R_1 \to -R_1 &: \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} \]

Gauss-Jordan: Worked Example (Part 2)

\[ \begin{aligned} R_2 \to R_2 - 4R_1 &: \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 4 & -3 \end{array}\right] \\[4pt] R_2 \to -R_2 &: \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -4 & 3 \end{array}\right] \\[4pt] R_1 \to R_1 - R_2 &: \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & 3 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & -4 & 3 \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} \]

\[ \mathbf{A}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Verify live if time allows: A A^-1 should equal I. Good comprehension check — ask a student to do the multiplication on the board.

When Inversion Breaks: Perfect Multicollinearity

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]

Row 3 is exactly row 1 \(+\) row 2 — no new information, exactly the dependence structure from Part I.

\[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 3 & 4 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \;\xrightarrow{R_3 \to R_3 - R_1 - R_2}\; \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -1 & -1 & 1 \end{array}\right] \]

A row of zeros on the left with a nonzero entry on the right. There is no way to turn row 3 into part of the identity — elimination cannot continue. \(\det(\mathbf{A})=0\); the inverse simply does not exist.

Near Multicollinearity: Barely Broken

Change one entry by \(0.1\) — row 3 is no longer exactly row 1 \(+\) row 2:

\[ \mathbf{A}_\varepsilon = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 & 4.1 \end{bmatrix} \]

\[ \det(\mathbf{A}_\varepsilon) = -0.3 \]

Nonzero — technically invertible. Small, though, compared to the determinants we have seen so far. What does “technically invertible but nearly singular” actually look like once we compute the inverse?

Near Multicollinearity: The Inverse Blows Up

\[ \mathbf{A}_\varepsilon^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 16.3 & 17.3 & -16.7 \\ -2.7 & -3.7 & 3.3 \\ -10 & -10 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \]

Nearly dependent rows produce a nearly-singular matrix — and a wildly inflated inverse. The determinant crossing zero is not a cliff edge; it’s the limit of a slope that gets steeper and steeper as \(\det(\mathbf{A}) \to 0\). We come back to why this matters numerically once we reach standard errors.

Properties of the Inverse

\[ (\mathbf{A}^{-1})^{-1} = \mathbf{A}, \qquad (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{-1} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}, \qquad (\mathbf{A}')^{-1} = (\mathbf{A}^{-1})' \]

Inverting a product reverses order — exactly the same pattern as the transpose in Part I. To undo “first \(\mathbf{A}\), then \(\mathbf{B}\),” you must undo \(\mathbf{B}\) first: \((\mathbf{AB})^{-1} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\), not \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\).

Only square, full-rank matrices have an inverse. A non-square matrix is never invertible — there is no such thing as “the inverse of a \(3\times 2\) matrix.”

Solving a Linear System

Section 2

From Equations to \(\mathbf{Ax} = \mathbf{b}\)

Three equations, three unknowns — the kind of small equilibrium system that shows up constantly in economics:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 2 \\ 2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 &= 8 \\ x_1 + 3x_2 + 2x_3 &= 11 \end{aligned} \]

In matrix form:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 8 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} \]

Any system of linear equations is \(\mathbf{Ax}=\mathbf{b}\): \(\mathbf{A}\) holds the coefficients, \(\mathbf{x}\) the unknowns, \(\mathbf{b}\) the right-hand side. This is the single most common matrix equation you will write all semester.

By Hand: Triangularize First

The idea: use row operations to eliminate variables one at a time — the same thing you would do by substitution, but systematic. Target an upper triangular system: zeros below the diagonal.

\[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 & 8 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 & 11 \end{array}\right] \]

\[ \begin{aligned} R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1 &: \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 & 11 \end{array}\right] \\[4pt] R_3 \to R_3 - R_1 &: \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 & 9 \end{array}\right] \\[4pt] R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_2 &: \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & -3 \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} \]

Triangular — every row now has at most one more nonzero entry than the row above it.

By Hand: Back-Substitute

The triangular system from the previous step:

\[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & -3 \end{array}\right] \]

Read it straight off, starting from the bottom row — it already has only one unknown:

\[ \begin{aligned} -x_3 &= -3 &&\Rightarrow \quad x_3 = 3 \\ x_2 + x_3 &= 4 &&\Rightarrow \quad x_2 = 4 - 3 = 1 \\ x_1 &= 2 &&\Rightarrow \quad x_1 = 2 \end{aligned} \]

\[ (x_1, x_2, x_3) = (2, 1, 3) \]

Back-substitution is why we triangularize. Once the system is triangular, each row hands you one unknown in terms of ones already solved — no simultaneous solving required, just substitute upward from the bottom.

The Alternative: Solve via the Inverse

Triangularization solves this system. What if tomorrow \(\mathbf{b}\) changes — new policy targets, a new forecast origin — but \(\mathbf{A}\) stays the same?

\[ \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b} \]

The same formula, every time, for any \(\mathbf{b}\). Once \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) is computed — by Gauss-Jordan, exactly as in Section 1 — solving for a new \(\mathbf{x}\) is one matrix multiplication, not a fresh triangularization from scratch.

This is also why software never triangularizes “by hand” the way we just did. A computer does not need the intuition of back-substitution — it needs a procedure that is identical regardless of the system’s size or structure. \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\) is that procedure: the same three symbols whether \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(3\times3\) or \(300\times300\).

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Section 3

What an Eigenvector Is

Most vectors, multiplied by a matrix, change both length and direction. Is there ever a direction a matrix leaves alone?

For most \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\) points somewhere new. But for special directions, \(\mathbf{A}\) only stretches or shrinks \(\mathbf{v}\) — it never rotates it.

Eigenvector / eigenvalue. \(\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}\) is an eigenvector of \(\mathbf{A}\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) if

\[ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \]

\(\mathbf{A}\) acting on \(\mathbf{v}\) is the same as just multiplying \(\mathbf{v}\) by the scalar \(\lambda\).

We will need this constantly once we study VAR stability and state-space models — a system is stable exactly when its eigenvalues satisfy a size condition we make precise later.

Finding Eigenvalues: The Characteristic Equation

Rearranging \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\):

\[ (\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \]

For a nonzero \(\mathbf{v}\) to solve this, \((\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\) must be singular — a direct callback to Part I.

Characteristic equation. \(\det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = 0\). For an \(n\times n\) matrix this is a degree-\(n\) polynomial in \(\lambda\) — up to \(n\) eigenvalues.

Eigenvalues: Worked Example

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

\[ \det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2-\lambda & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix} \]

Expanding along the middle row (mostly zeros — the easy row to expand on):

\[ \begin{aligned} \det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}) &= (2-\lambda)\Big[(2-\lambda)^2 - 1\Big] \\ &= -\lambda^3 + 6\lambda^2 - 11\lambda + 6 \end{aligned} \]

Setting this to zero and factoring:

\[ \lambda^3 - 6\lambda^2 + 11\lambda - 6 = (\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3) = 0 \]

\[ \lambda_1 = 1, \qquad \lambda_2 = 2, \qquad \lambda_3 = 3 \]

Eigenvectors: \(\lambda_1 = 1\)

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{A} - 1\cdot\mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Row 3 is identical to row 1 — the linear combination is \(\text{row 3} = 1\cdot(\text{row 1})\), confirming \(\det(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I})=0\) exactly as the characteristic equation requires.

Reading the two independent rows as equations: row 1 gives \(v_1+v_3=0\); row 2 gives \(v_2=0\).

\[ \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Eigenvectors: \(\lambda_2 = 2\)

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{A} - 2\cdot\mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Row 2 is entirely zero — the linear combination is \(\text{row 2} = 0\cdot(\text{row 1}) + 0\cdot(\text{row 3})\), the simplest possible dependence, and again confirms singularity.

Row 1 gives \(v_3=0\); row 3 gives \(v_1=0\) — \(v_2\) is free.

\[ \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Eigenvectors: \(\lambda_3 = 3\)

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{A} - 3\cdot\mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Row 3 is \(-1\times\) row 1 — the linear combination is \(\text{row 3} = -1\cdot(\text{row 1})\), again singular by construction.

Row 1 gives \(-v_1+v_3=0\); row 2 gives \(v_2=0\).

\[ \mathbf{v}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Eigenvectors are not unique — any nonzero multiple of \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_3\) is equally valid. Software reports a normalized (unit-length) version; the direction is what matters, not the scale.

Why Eigenvalues Matter Here

The preview, not the whole story. Later in the course, a VAR or a state-space model is stable exactly when the eigenvalues of its coefficient matrix are small enough in absolute value (formally: modulus less than one). One number per direction — \(\lambda\) — tells us whether shocks fade out or explode. That is the entire reason this section exists in a forecasting course.

We are not proving stability conditions today — just building the vocabulary so that “the eigenvalues of the companion matrix” is not a new phrase when it shows up in Chapter 7.

Deriving and Computing OLS

Section 4

The Payoff Section

Back to the very first slide of Part I:

OLS itself collapses from page-long summation formulas to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\)

We now have every tool needed to actually derive this claim — not just state it — and to see exactly why it scales in a way the scalar approach does not.

Two derivations, same destination: minimize the sum of squared residuals. One route gets harder with every added regressor. The other does not change shape at all.

Deriving OLS the Scalar Way: Set Up the Problem

One regressor, \(n\) observations. The residual for observation \(i\):

\[ e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i \]

Ordinary least squares. Choose \(\beta_0,\beta_1\) to minimize the sum of squared residuals:

\[ S(\beta_0,\beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2 \]

Two unknowns, so we need two first-order conditions — set both partial derivatives to zero.

Deriving OLS the Scalar Way: First-Order Conditions

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta_0} = -2\sum_i (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i) = 0 \]

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \beta_1} = -2\sum_i x_i(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i) = 0 \]

Rearranging both gives the normal equations — two linear equations in \(\beta_0,\beta_1\), next slide.

Deriving OLS the Scalar Way: The Normal Equations

\[ \begin{aligned} n\beta_0 + \beta_1\sum_i x_i &= \sum_i y_i \\ \beta_0\sum_i x_i + \beta_1\sum_i x_i^2 &= \sum_i x_i y_i \end{aligned} \]

Two linear equations, two unknowns — solvable by substitution:

\[ \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2}, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1\bar{x} \]

This is already two normal equations from two regressors (\(1\) and \(x\)). A third regressor means three normal equations in three unknowns, solved by substitution or Cramer’s rule. A fourth means four — and by the time a model has eight or ten controls, this is no longer something anyone does by hand. The algebra does not scale.

Deriving OLS in Matrix Form: Set Up the Problem

Same minimization, written for any number of regressors at once. Stack every observation:

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{e}, \qquad \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \]

\(\mathbf{X}\) is \(n\times k\) — \(n\) observations, \(k\) regressors (including the intercept column). \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is \(k\times 1\), whatever \(k\) happens to be.

\[ S(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{e}'\mathbf{e} = (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})'(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]

One scalar objective, regardless of \(k\). Whether \(\mathbf{X}\) has two columns or twenty, \(\mathbf{e}'\mathbf{e}\) is always a \((1\times n)(n\times 1) \to 1\times 1\) — a single number — because conformability guarantees it.

Deriving OLS in Matrix Form: Expanding

\[ \begin{aligned} S(\boldsymbol{\beta}) &= (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})'(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \\ &= \mathbf{y}'\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}'\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \\ &= \mathbf{y}'\mathbf{y} - 2\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \end{aligned} \]

The middle two terms are each scalars, and a scalar always equals its own transpose: \(\mathbf{y}'\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\mathbf{y}'\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})' = \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\). That is what collapses \(-\mathbf{y}'\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} - \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\) into \(-2\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\).

Deriving OLS in Matrix Form: The First-Order Condition

Differentiate with respect to the vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) and set to zero — one matrix equation replaces \(k\) separate scalar equations:

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = -2\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} + 2\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0} \]

\[ \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \]

These are the normal equations from the scalar case — \(k\) of them, packaged into one matrix equation. Now solve exactly as we solved \(\mathbf{Ax}=\mathbf{b}\) in Section 2: left-multiply by \((\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\).

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} \]

This is the whole point. One formula, one derivation, works for \(k=2\) or \(k=20\). The scalar route needs a fresh, larger system of normal equations every time a regressor is added. The matrix route needs nothing new — just a bigger \(\mathbf{X}\).

Applying Both Routes to the Same Data

Four observations, one regressor:

\(i\) \(x_i\) \(y_i\)
1 1 3
2 2 5
3 3 6
4 4 9

A minimal dataset — small enough to carry through both routes by hand in full.

Route 1: Scalar Formula Applied

With \(\bar{x}=2.5\), \(\bar{y}=5.75\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}) &= (-1.5)(-2.75)+(-0.5)(-0.75)+(0.5)(0.25)+(1.5)(3.25) = 9.5 \\ \sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2 &= 2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25 = 5 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{9.5}{5} = 1.9, \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 = 5.75 - (1.9)(2.5) = 1.0 \]

Route 2: Matrix Formula Applied — Building the Pieces

\[ \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \\ 6 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

\[ \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 10 \\ 10 & 30 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 23 \\ 67 \end{bmatrix} \]

The column of ones in \(\mathbf{X}\) is exactly what produces the row/column sums of \(1\), \(x_i\), and \(x_i^2\) that the scalar formula computes term by term. Same information, packaged differently.

Route 2: Matrix Formula Applied — Inverting and Solving

\[ \det(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}) = (4)(30) - (10)(10) = 20 \]

\(2\times2\) shortcut, \(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}\) — safe by hand only at \(2\times2\); larger cases use Gauss-Jordan as in Section 1:

\[ (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{bmatrix} 30 & -10 \\ -10 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & -0.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} \]

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & -0.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 23 \\ 67 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 \\ 1.9 \end{bmatrix} \]

\(\hat{\beta}_0 = 1.0\), \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 1.9\) — identical to Route 1. Two derivations, one algebraic and one that scales, arriving at the same number.

Standard Errors: Where \((\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) Earns Its Keep

The matrix route does not stop at \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) — it hands us standard errors for free, using the inverse we already computed:

Variance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\).

\[ \widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \hat{\sigma}^2 (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\sum_i \hat{e}_i^2}{n-k} \]

where \(\hat{e}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) are the residuals and \(k\) the number of estimated coefficients. (You will derive this formally in econometrics; here we only need the mechanical link to \((\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\).)

Fitted values \(\hat{y}_i = (2.9,\, 4.8,\, 6.7,\, 8.6)\), residuals \(\hat{e}_i = (0.1,\, 0.2,\, -0.7,\, 0.4)\), giving \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = 0.35\).

\[ \widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = 0.35\begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & -0.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.525 & -0.175 \\ -0.175 & 0.070 \end{bmatrix} \]

\[ \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_0) = \sqrt{0.525} \approx 0.72, \qquad \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \sqrt{0.070} \approx 0.26 \]

Reading the Variance-Covariance Matrix

The variance-covariance matrix of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) is always symmetric, \(k\times k\):

  • Diagonal — \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_j)\); its square root is \(\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_j)\), the number every regression table reports
  • Off-diagonal — \(\text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_i,\hat{\beta}_j)\); how estimation error in one coefficient moves with estimation error in another

Here, \(\text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_0,\hat{\beta}_1) = -0.175\). Dividing by both standard errors gives the correlation of their sampling errors:

\[ \text{Corr}(\hat{\beta}_0,\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{-0.175}{(0.72)(0.26)} \approx -0.91 \]

Why the sign makes sense. Push the fitted line’s intercept up and, to keep fitting the same data, its slope typically has to come down — the two coefficients are not estimated independently. This off-diagonal term is why joint hypothesis tests on several coefficients at once need the whole variance-covariance matrix, not just a list of individual standard errors.

Closing the Loop: Multicollinearity and Standard Errors

Recall \(\mathbf{A}_\varepsilon^{-1}\) from Section 1 — entries in the teens, from a determinant of just \(-0.3\).

Now replace \(\mathbf{A}_\varepsilon\) with \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\). If two regressors are nearly collinear, \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) is nearly singular — its determinant is close to zero, and \((\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\) has huge entries, exactly as we saw. Since \(\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \hat{\sigma}^2(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\), that inflation passes straight through: standard errors explode, \(t\)-statistics collapse toward zero, and coefficients that may be economically large look statistically insignificant.

This is the mechanical reason multicollinearity produces the textbook symptom — “high \(R^2\), but nothing individually significant.” It was never a separate phenomenon; it is the same singularity problem from Section 1, one formula later.

Recap

We computed the matrix inverse by Gauss-Jordan, saw exactly how and why it fails under multicollinearity, solved a full linear system both by hand triangularization and by inverse, found eigenvalues and eigenvectors by hand, then derived OLS two ways — scalar and matrix — confirming they agree, reading the variance-covariance matrix, and tracing standard errors back to the same inverse that broke down under collinearity.
